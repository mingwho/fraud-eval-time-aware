\begin{abstract}
Fraud-detection studies on public payment data commonly evaluate with a random train/test split, and some oversample before splitting. Both let information from the test set reach the model. We measure how much this inflates results by training five standard classifiers (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM and a small neural network) under random and chronological splits of the IEEE-CIS and ULB credit card datasets, with identical tuning budgets and training-set sizes. On IEEE-CIS, the area under the precision--recall curve falls by \num{ieee.min.drop.pr} to \num{ieee.max.drop.pr} (\num{ieee.min.rel.pr}--\num{ieee.max.rel.pr}\%) when models must predict later transactions; at a 5\% alert budget the best model catches \num{ieee.lgbm.chrono.r05} of fraud cases instead of the \num{ieee.lgbm.random.r05} the random split promises. Controls that score both protocols on the same transactions show that this is leakage and not a harder test period: under a random split, \num{ieee.key.fraud.random}\% of test fraud cases come from a card with fraud in the training data, against \num{ieee.key.fraud.chrono}\% under a chronological split. On the two-day ULB dataset the same controls attribute most of an apparent drop to a harder final period, and its estimates are too noisy to rank models. Applying SMOTE before splitting is the larger problem there: non-linear models reach a precision--recall AUC of up to \num{ulb.max.leakreal.pr} on genuine test transactions, against at most \num{ulb.max.smote.pr} when only training data are oversampled. We give a checklist for evaluating fraud models and release the code.
\end{abstract}
