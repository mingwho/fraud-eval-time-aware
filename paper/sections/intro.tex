\section{Introduction}

Payment fraud is a natural application of supervised learning: labelled transactions are plentiful, the cost of an error is measurable, and a model's score can be turned directly into a decision to block or review. Public datasets have made the problem accessible, and two of them, the credit card dataset from Universit\'e Libre de Bruxelles (ULB)~\cite{dalpozzolo2015calibrating} and the IEEE-CIS competition dataset~\cite{ieeecis2019fraud}, now underpin a large body of published comparisons.

A deployed fraud model is trained on the past and scores the future. Much of the published work evaluates differently. Transactions are shuffled and split at random into training and test sets, or into cross-validation folds~\cite{taha2020intelligent,esenogho2022neural,alfaiz2022enhanced,mim2024soft,du2024novel}, so that the model is tested on transactions that are interleaved in time with the ones it was trained on. Some pipelines also rebalance the classes with SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote} before splitting~\cite{ileberi2021performance,khalid2024enhancing}, so that synthetic copies of test fraud cases end up in the training set. Both are forms of data leakage~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}, and the accuracies above 99\% that often result say little about how a model would perform in production.

That random splits flatter fraud models has been noted before~\cite{grover2022fraud,breskuviene2024enhancing,hayat2025data}. What has been missing is a controlled measurement of how much, for which models, and why. This paper provides one. We train five standard classifiers on both public datasets under a random split and under chronological protocols, holding the tuning budget, the training-set size and everything else fixed, so that the difference in scores can be attributed to the split.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A like-for-like measurement of split inflation.} On IEEE-CIS, moving from a random to a chronological split lowers \prauc{} by \num{ieee.min.rel.pr}--\num{ieee.max.rel.pr}\% for every model, consistently across rolling windows. We add controls that score both protocols on the same transactions, and they matter: they confirm that the IEEE-CIS gap is leakage, and they show that most of the apparent gap on ULB is a harder final period.
\item \textbf{The oversampling leak, separated into its two parts.} We distinguish the artefact of scoring a rebalanced test set from genuine information flow. The second is large for flexible models and absent for logistic regression.
\item \textbf{Metrics tied to an alert budget.} We report the share of fraud cases and of fraud value caught when a fixed share of transactions can be reviewed, and show where in the ranking the inflation sits.
\item \textbf{An explanation and a checklist.} We trace the inflation to fraud episodes that span the split: the same card, minutes apart, on both sides. We condense the findings into a checklist for authors and reviewers, and release code that reproduces every number.
\end{itemize}
