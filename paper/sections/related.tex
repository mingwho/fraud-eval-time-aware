\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Machine learning for card fraud}
Card fraud detection is a long-studied supervised learning problem characterized by rare positives, changing behavior and delayed labels, and recent reviews cover the range of methods that have been applied to it~\cite{lucas2020credit}. A recurring finding is that features aggregating a card's recent history improve detection substantially~\cite{bahnsen2016feature,jurgovsky2018sequence}. Dal Pozzolo and colleagues, working with an industrial partner, described what a realistic evaluation involves~\cite{dalpozzolo2014learned,dalpozzolo2018credit}. Fraud patterns drift over time, labels are immediate only for the few transactions that investigators review and arrive late for the rest, and the operationally relevant quantity is the precision of the small set of alerts raised each day. The practical handbook by Le Borgne et al.~\cite{leborgne2022fraud} translates the same principles into a procedure with training and test sets ordered in time, separated by a delay period, and prequential validation. Our chronological, gap and rolling protocols follow this line of work.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice on public fraud datasets}
Many studies on the ULB and IEEE-CIS datasets do not evaluate in this way. Random, shuffled or stratified train/test splits and shuffled $k$-fold cross-validation are common~\cite{esenogho2022neural,alfaiz2022enhanced,chung2023credit,mim2024soft,du2024novel,becerrasuarez2026conservative}, and some papers describe resampling the whole dataset before dividing it into training and test sets~\cite{ileberi2021performance,ahmad2023class,khalid2024enhancing}. The reported figures are often close to perfect, for example an accuracy of 99.98\% on ULB~\cite{ileberi2021performance}. We do not know how prevalent these practices are, since we found no audit that counts them, only qualitative critiques. Hayat and Magnier~\cite{hayat2025data} review twenty methods evaluated on ULB, find pre-split preprocessing and oversampling in many of them, and show that a trivial network inside a leaky pipeline matches published results. Kabane~\cite{kabane2024impact} compares sampling before and after the split for XGBoost on ULB. Each of these papers identifies the missing temporal split as a problem but does not test it.

\paragraph{Leakage and time-aware evaluation}
Leakage, the use of information during training that would not be available at prediction time, was formalized by Kaufman et al.~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}. Kapoor and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023leakage} document it across many scientific fields, and their taxonomy includes both issues studied here, namely preprocessing or sampling before the split and test data that do not respect time. Santos et al.~\cite{santos2018cross} show that oversampling before cross-validation yields over-optimistic estimates on imbalanced data in general. In security, TESSERACT~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract} demonstrated that malware classifiers evaluated without temporal constraints appear far better than they perform on future samples. Blocked and forward-chaining validation for temporally dependent data is well established in forecasting~\cite{bergmeir2012use}.

\paragraph{Closest prior work}
The observation that random splits produce optimistic estimates for fraud models is not new, and we do not claim it as a contribution. The Fraud Dataset Benchmark~\cite{grover2022fraud} provides out-of-time test sets for several public datasets, including both of ours, and reports that the validation \rocauc{} of AutoML tools overstates their out-of-time \rocauc{} markedly on IEEE-CIS and only slightly on ULB. Georgiev et al.~\cite{georgiev2026modeling} rerun one convolutional network on ULB under a chronological split in an appendix and observe recall falling from 0.88 to 0.80. Breskuvien\.{e} and Dzemyda~\cite{breskuviene2024enhancing} adopt a time-based split for a feature-selection study and argue against random splitting. Hsin et al.~\cite{hsin2022feature} compare chronological and random partitioning on proprietary fund-transfer data and find the random results unrealistically high.

None of these studies provides a controlled, like-for-like measurement on both public datasets with the same models, tuning budget and training-set size under both protocols, together with a quantification of the oversampling leak, controls for test-period difficulty, and alert-budget metrics, which is what this paper contributes.
